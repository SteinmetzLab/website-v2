A decision takes less than a second. We are learning to watch it happen.

In the time it takes to notice something and act on it, signals cross dozens of regions of the brain. Until recently, no one could see more than a sliver of that. We can now watch thousands of neurons at once, across the whole brain, while an animal decides.

The question

Where in the brain does a decision actually get made?

The honest answer, for most of the history of neuroscience, has been that we did not know — not because the question was uninteresting, but because we could only listen to a handful of cells at a time, in one place, and hope we had chosen the right place.

That constraint has lifted. New recording methods let us follow activity across the entire brain simultaneously, at the resolution of single cells and single milliseconds. The result has been humbling: signals we expected to find in one specialised region turn out to be almost everywhere, and the interesting question becomes not where a signal exists but where it matters.

These are the same circuits that falter in Parkinson's disease, in schizophrenia, in the failures of attention that follow a stroke. Understanding how they work while they are working is the prerequisite for understanding how they fail.

The work

Three things had to be invented before this was possible.

The first is the probe. A silicon shank thinner than a human hair, carrying hundreds of recording sites, that can be lowered through the brain and listen to every cell it passes. We helped design and test these, and we build the software the rest of the field uses to make sense of what they record.

The second is a way of seeing the whole surface at once, so that anything found in one location can be placed in the context of everything else happening at that moment.

The third is the ability to interrupt. Watching tells you what a circuit does; switching it off, briefly and precisely, tells you whether it mattered. Correlation is abundant in the brain. Causation has to be earned.

Support this work

Federal funding decides what is safe. Private support decides what is possible.

Grants are awarded for work that can be justified in advance. The findings we are proudest of came from experiments we could not have promised would work. Philanthropic support is what lets a laboratory take that kind of risk — and what lets a graduate student follow an unexpected result rather than abandon it.

  • A named graduate fellowship frees a student from teaching obligations for a year and lets them commit to a single hard problem.
  • Instrumentation — a recording rig, a microscope — outlasts any individual grant and serves every project that follows.
  • Unrestricted support is the rarest and most valuable, because it can be spent on the idea that has not been justified yet.
